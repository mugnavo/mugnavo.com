Before we design anything, we ask a few questions. None of them are hard, but a project goes a lot smoother when the answers exist before the first call instead of trickling in halfway through.

This is that list, plus a few things people tend to prepare that you can skip.

## What the site needs to do

Not "look modern" or "be better than the current one". Something you could check a few months after launch.

For a clinic, that might be more appointment requests and fewer phone calls asking about opening hours. For a startup, it might be more demo bookings, or a page you can send to investors that explains the product without you there to walk them through it.

Pick one goal, two at most. It decides what goes on the homepage, which pages exist at all, and what every page is nudging people toward.

Then think about who's on the other end. Someone who's never heard of you needs different things from an existing customer looking for your support email. If you can name the two or three kinds of visitors that matter most, and what each of them came to do, you've already done a good part of the planning.

## What you already have

Gather whatever exists, even the parts you're not happy with:

- logo files (an SVG or the original design file, not a screenshot of your Facebook header)
- brand colors and fonts, if you have them
- current copy, including anything on your old site worth keeping
- photos of your team, product, space, or work
- testimonials you have permission to use

Rough is fine. What we're really after is knowing what's missing. No photos means planning a shoot, or a design that doesn't depend on them. That's easy to plan around in the first week and stressful to discover the week before launch.

## Who has the keys

List every account the site touches: the domain, hosting, email, analytics, forms, payments, booking tools. Next to each one, write down who can log in.

This tends to be messier than people expect. The domain might be registered under someone who left the company years ago, or analytics might be tied to a personal Gmail account. None of it is hard to fix, but fixing it usually means waiting on someone else, so it's worth starting early.

## Budget, timeline, and who decides

A rough budget range is enough, and so is "we'd like it live before our March launch". Both let us suggest a scope that fits instead of guessing. Sometimes the honest answer is a smaller first version that launches on time, with the rest added after.

Also decide who gives the final yes. Lots of people can give feedback, and they should. But when three people each have the final say, the result tends to be a compromise none of them really wanted.

## What you can skip

Some things feel like good preparation but don't help much at this stage:

- **A long brief.** A page of honest notes is more useful than twenty pages of requirements. We'll ask follow-up questions either way.
- **A finished sitemap.** You know your business, and we know how sites tend to be structured. It's better worked out together.
- **Final copy.** Writing every page before the structure exists usually means rewriting it later. Drafts and notes are plenty.
